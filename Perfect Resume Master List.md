# The Perfect Resume: Master List

Nov 15, 2026 · @DJ

A resume has two readers, a parser and a skimming human, and every rule below exists to serve one of them. Most thresholds quoted here come from resume-tool vendors and career sites, so treat the numbers as working guidance, not laws.

## 1. How a resume is actually read

1. **Application form.** Knockout questions (work authorization, location, required licence) run first. This is the one place automatic rejection is routine.
2. **Parsing.** The ATS strips the file to plain text and sorts it into fields: contact, job titles, employers, dates, skills, education.
3. **Storage and ranking.** The parsed record goes into a searchable database. Many teams sort or filter it by match score or keyword search.
4. **Recruiter skim.** The first pass is a fit / no-fit glance of about 6 seconds ([TheLadders eye-tracking study](https://www.marketingprofs.com/charts/2014/25190/eye-tracking-study-how-recruiters-view-resumes), 30 recruiters). Almost 80% of that time goes to name, current title and company, previous title and company, dates, and education.
5. **Full read.** Only resumes that survive the skim get read properly, usually by the hiring manager.

What follows from this:

- If parsing breaks, the recruiter sees a half-empty record and has no reason to suspect it is wrong.
- The skim follows a fixed visual path, so titles, companies and dates must sit where the eye expects them.
- Detail copy barely affects the first decision. It decides the second.

## 2. How resumes are scored

There is no single universal score. Each employer's system and each checker tool computes its own, and many employers do not use one at all.

What the evidence says ([Enhancv interviews with 25 US recruiters](https://enhancv.com/blog/does-ats-reject-resumes/), Sept–Oct 2025; small sample, run by a resume-tool vendor):

- 92% (23 of 25) said their ATS does not auto-reject for formatting, content or design.
- 8% (2 of 25) had content-based auto-reject switched on (match thresholds or required-skill counts).
- 44% said their ATS shows an AI or fit score. 36% use it as a guide and still check manually. 56% ignore it or do not have it.
- 52% said applying early helps because they review in order of arrival.
- 20% called over-designed, Canva-style resumes a turn-off because they are harder to scan.

What typically feeds a match score:

- Overlap between the job description's terms and your resume (hard skills, tools, job titles).
- Where the terms appear: title, summary and recent experience count for more than a bare list.
- How complete the parsed record is: full work history, dated timeline, clear skills list. Empty fields read as gaps.
- Whether the system matches literally or by meaning. Vendor write-ups describe older systems as literal keyword matchers and newer ones as context-aware, so write for the literal case.

What a checker-tool score is:

- An estimate by that tool of keyword overlap with one job description. The employer never sees it.
- Commonly quoted target: around 75–80% match. Useful as a gap-finder, pointless to chase to 100%.

The human score:

- Recruiters tick requirements off a list, sometimes rating each 1–5.
- What they said they want, by share who mentioned it: skimmable structure 92%, relevant experience and skills 88%, natural keyword use 76%, short bullets 72%, simple consistent formatting 68%, one to two pages 64%, measurable achievements 52%.

Cheapest points, in order:

1. Answer knockout questions correctly.
2. Parse cleanly (section 5).
3. Use the posting's exact job title and must-have skills in its wording.
4. Apply within the first few days.
5. Put the strongest matching evidence in the top third of page one.

## 3. Types of resume

Use reverse-chronological or hybrid. Avoid pure functional.

| Type | Leads with | Use when | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Reverse-chronological | Most recent role, then backwards | Steady history in the target field. The default recruiters expect | Exposes gaps and unrelated roles |
| Hybrid (combination) | Skills block, then full dated history | Students, career changers, gaps, mixed freelance and project work | Skills block can turn into an unproven list |
| Functional (skills-based) | Skills grouped by theme, history reduced to a list | Almost never | Parsers cannot tie skills to jobs, and recruiters assume something is hidden |
| Academic CV | Education, research, publications | Research roles, academic applications abroad | Too long for industry jobs |
| Designed / creative | Visual layout | Design roles, sent directly to a person | Breaks parsing. Keep a plain version for portals |

Sources: [Robert Half](https://www.roberthalf.com/blog/writing-a-resume/which-resume-format-is-best-for-you), [FlexJobs](https://www.flexjobs.com/blog/post/6-tips-for-using-a-creative-resume), [Coursera](https://www-cloudfront-alias.coursera.org/articles/types-of-resumes). The last two rows are general practice, not from those pages.

- "Targeted resume" is not a format. It is any of the above rewritten for one posting (section 8).
- For a student with projects, freelance work and hackathon wins, hybrid fits best: skills, then projects and experience with dates.

## 4. Structure

One page, one column, strongest evidence first. Indian fresher guides agree on one page and no photo; they differ on exact order, so order by what you have.

Section order, student or early-career tech:

1. Header: name, target title, phone, email, city, LinkedIn, GitHub, portfolio
2. Summary: 2 lines, optional, only if it says something specific
3. Skills: grouped (Languages, Frameworks, Tools, Cloud)
4. Experience: internships, freelance and client work, with dates
5. Projects: 2–4, each with stack, link and outcome
6. Education: degree, college, year, CGPA
7. Achievements: hackathon wins, competitions, certifications, open source

Section order, 2+ years experience: Header, Summary, Experience, Skills, Projects, Education.

Rules per section:

- **Header.** In the document body, never in the page header or footer, which parsers often skip. City only, no full address.
- **Headings.** Standard words: Experience, Projects, Skills, Education. Not "Career Journey" or "What I've Built".
- **Each entry.** Title, organisation, location, dates on one or two lines in the same pattern every time.
- **Dates.** One format throughout (for example Jun 2025 – Aug 2025).
- **Skills.** Real, specific tools only. If you cannot answer interview questions on it, cut it.
- **Projects.** Shipped work only. Each needs a live link or repo.
- **Education.** Show CGPA on its scale (8.4/10). One guide suggests omitting it below 7.

Leave out:

- Photo, date of birth, father's name, religion, marital status
- Declaration line and signature
- Career-objective clichés
- Hobbies and soft-skill paragraphs used as padding
- Skill bars, star ratings, percentages for skills
- "References available on request"

Length:

- Under roughly 5 years of experience: one page.
- Beyond that: two pages only if page two holds real delivered work.

Sources: [GoodSpace](https://goodspace.ai/resume-examples/engineering-fresher), [Naukri](https://www.naukri.com/career-advice/how-to-make-resume-for-freshers), [StylingCV India](https://stylingcv.com/in/?p=5), [JobRise](https://jobrise.io/blog/one-page-resume-guide-india).

## 5. Raw design

Good resume design is typography and spacing only: one column, one font family, clear hierarchy, nothing decorative.

| Element | Setting |
| --- | --- |
| Layout | Single column, top to bottom, left-aligned |
| Font family | One family for everything; bold for emphasis |
| Safe fonts | Calibri, Arial, Helvetica, Georgia, Garamond, Cambria; Lato or Roboto for a tech feel |
| Body size | 10–12 pt, 11 pt ideal, never below 10 |
| Section headings | 12–16 pt, bold |
| Name | 16–24 pt |
| Line spacing | 1.0–1.15 |
| Margins | 1 in default, 0.5 in floor, equal on opposite sides |
| Bullets | Standard • or - only |
| Colour | Black text; at most one restrained accent on headings |
| File | Text-based PDF, or DOCX if the posting asks |

Font, size and margin ranges: [Enhancv](https://enhancv.com/blog/ats-friendly-fonts), [AIResume.guru](https://airesume.guru/blog/resume-margins-fonts-spacing), [Made Good Designs](https://madegooddesigns.com/best-fonts-for-resumes/). The colour row is my recommendation.

Hierarchy, in the order the eye should land:

1. Name
2. Section headings
3. Job title and organisation (bold)
4. Dates (same position on every entry)
5. Bullet text

Spacing:

- More space between sections than between entries, and more between entries than between bullets.
- Same gap for the same kind of break, everywhere.
- If it does not fit, cut content before shrinking type or margins.

What breaks parsing:

- Two columns or sidebars built with tables or text boxes: the parser reads across the row and interleaves sections
- Tables used for layout, including skills tables
- Text boxes and shapes: often ignored entirely
- Contact details in the page header or footer
- Icons, images, logos, headshots, skill bars
- Image-based PDFs (scans, flattened design-tool exports)
- Decorative, script or condensed display fonts
- All-caps paragraphs; underlining anything except links

Sources for the breakers: [RecruitBPM](https://recruitbpm.com/?p=20244), [JobWizard](https://jobwizard.ai/blog/how-to-optimize-your-resume-for-ats-systems-in-2026-872842), [Adobe](https://www.adobe.com/acrobat/resources/resume-formatting-mistakes.html), [JobShinobi](https://jobshinobi.com/blog/how-to-create-an-ats-friendly-pdf-resume).

How to test the design:

- Select all in the PDF, paste into a plain-text editor. The order and content should read correctly.
- Upload to LinkedIn's resume import and check which fields it fills.
- View at 100% zoom on a phone and print one copy.

## 6. Keywords

Keywords come from the job description, and they belong inside evidence, not only in a list.

Where to find them:

- The job title itself
- The "required" and "must have" lines
- Tools, languages, frameworks, platforms named
- Certifications and degrees named
- Nouns and phrases the posting repeats
- Pull the 10 that matter most; ignore the rest

How to write them:

- Use the posting's exact phrase. If it says "Google Analytics", do not write "web analytics tools".
- Give both forms once where an acronym exists: "Search Engine Optimization (SEO)".
- Only claim what you can defend in an interview.

Where to put them:

1. Headline under your name (the target job title)
2. Summary
3. Skills section
4. Inside experience and project bullets, next to a result

Priority when space is tight: hard skills and tools, then job titles, then domain terms, then soft skills.

Never:

- Stuff a keyword list with things you have not used
- Hide white text
- Paste the job description into the file
- Repeat one term until the sentence reads unnaturally

Sources: [Mirrai](https://mirrai-career.pages.dev/insights/how-to-tailor-resume-to-job-description), [Scale.jobs](https://blog.scale.jobs/how-to-match-resume-keywords-to-job-descriptions), [AIResume.guru](https://airesume.guru/blog/how-to-tailor-your-resume-to-a-job-description-the-6x-interview-multiplier). The acronym rule, priority order and "never" list are standard practice, not from those pages.

## 7. Writing the content

Every bullet states a result, its measure, and how you got it. The formula from Laszlo Bock, Google's former head of People Operations: Accomplished \[X\] as measured by \[Y\] by doing \[Z\] ([his article](https://www.calstatela.edu/sites/default/files/formula_for_a_winning_resume.docx)).

The same bullet at three levels (illustrative numbers):

1. Weak: Responsible for the inventory system
2. Better: Built an inventory system for a stationery retailer
3. Strong: Cut stock-entry time by 40% for a 3-store retailer by building a barcode-driven inventory app in Next.js and PostgreSQL

Bullet rules:

- Start with an active verb: Built, Shipped, Cut, Grew, Automated, Led, Designed
- Never "Responsible for", "Worked on", "Helped with"
- One idea per bullet, 1–2 lines
- 2–4 bullets per role or project; most recent and most relevant get the most
- Name the stack inside the bullet
- No "I", no full stops needed, past tense for finished work

Where numbers come from when you think you have none:

- Scale: users, orders, records, stores, requests per day
- Speed: load time, hours saved, turnaround
- Money: revenue, cost saved, client budget
- Rank: 1st of N teams, top X%
- Scope: team size, duration, number of clients
- Give a baseline where you can: from what, to what

Project entries:

- Name, one-line purpose, stack, link
- What you personally built
- What happened because of it (users, win, client adoption)

Accuracy:

- Every number must be one you can explain in an interview.
- Zero typos. Read it aloud once and have one other person read it.

## 8. Specificity

One master file holds everything; each application gets a one-page cut built for that posting. Tailoring guides put this at roughly 10 minutes per application once the master exists.

Keep:

- A master resume: every role, project, win and bullet variant, any length, never sent
- One base version per role family (for example: software engineer, product designer, marketing)

Change per application:

1. Headline: the posting's job title
2. Summary: 2 lines rewritten around its top requirements
3. Skills: reordered so the posting's stack comes first
4. Top bullets under each entry: swapped for the ones that match
5. Projects: pick the 2–4 closest to the role
6. Missing keywords: added only where true

Do not change: dates, titles, numbers, facts.

Focus on the top third of page one. That is where the skim lands.

## 9. Build workflow and pre-send checklist

Build order:

1. Dump everything into the master file
2. Rewrite every bullet to result, measure, method
3. Pick the format (section 3) and section order (section 4)
4. Set the design once (section 5) and save it as a template
5. Collect 5–10 target postings and extract the recurring keywords
6. Cut to one page for the first role family
7. Run the parse tests
8. Tailor per application

Before every send:

- [ ] One page, one column
- [ ] Headline matches the posting's job title
- [ ] Top 10 keywords from the posting appear, in its wording
- [ ] Every bullet starts with a verb and most carry a number
- [ ] Contact details in the body; links work
- [ ] Dates in one format
- [ ] One font family, body 10–12 pt
- [ ] No tables, text boxes, icons, images or photo
- [ ] Plain-text paste reads in the right order
- [ ] Text-based PDF, named Firstname-Lastname-Role.pdf
- [ ] Read aloud once; zero typos
- [ ] LinkedIn and portfolio say the same things as the resume
- [ ] Knockout questions on the form answered accurately

## 10. Myths to ignore

| Myth | What the sources show |
| --- | --- |
| "75% of resumes are auto-rejected by the ATS" | No study behind it; [traced](https://resumegeni.com/blog/why-92-percent-recruiters-say-ats-doesnt-auto-reject) to a defunct vendor's marketing claim. Recruiter interviews point to volume, not software |
| "You get 6 seconds, so content does not matter" | The 6 seconds is the [first sorting glance only](https://www.hiration.com/blog/how-long-recruiters-spend-on-resume/). Content decides everything after it |
| "You need a 100% ATS score" | Checker scores are one tool's estimate. Most recruiters in the Enhancv sample ignore fit scores or treat them as a guide |
| "A striking design makes you stand out" | 5 of 25 recruiters called Canva-style resumes a turn-off; 92% asked for skimmable structure |
| "PDFs cannot be parsed" | Text-based PDFs are the usual recommendation. Image-based PDFs are the problem |
| "More keywords is always better" | 76% of recruiters asked for natural keyword use, not stuffing |
| "Functional format hides gaps" | It signals them. Hybrid is the recommended alternative |
